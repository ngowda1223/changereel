Approving a change you were never shown

The description says the new address check now catches apartment numbers. It probably does. Seeing it means pulling the branch, building it, installing it, then hunting for a test account that reaches that screen in the right state. There is a queue of changes behind this one. So it gets approved on the description, which is how most changes get approved.

Changereel does that work and attaches the result to the change itself. It reads the merged difference, works out which screens it touches, drives the real build until it reaches them, and sets up whatever state is needed to get there. What comes back is the change happening, on the actual screen. If it cannot reach that state, it says so rather than showing something that looks close.

Under the hood

Why you can trust Changereel

A recording proves nothing on its own. Every reel is a claim that has to survive checks before it is allowed to exist.

  • Every tap is confirmed by the device, not assumed.The runner compares a pixel signature before and after each interaction. A tap on a disabled control produces no visible response, so it fails loudly instead of continuing into a flow that never advanced.
  • Every scene is gated on the screen it claims.Before narration plays, the accessibility tree is polled for text the app map extracted from the source for that screen. Land somewhere else and the run stops.
  • Every recording is checked for motion.Frames are sampled and hashed across the take. A file that is technically valid but visually frozen is refused rather than shipped as a demo.
  • Nothing of yours reaches us.The whole pipeline runs in your environment. Source goes only to the model provider whose account you are using, and we have no access to your repository, your builds, or the reels it produces. What we learn about your app, we learn because you chose to tell us.
  • Elements are targeted the way the app already describes itself.resource-id, visible text, or content description. No SDK to install, no test hooks to add, nothing to maintain as the interface changes.

When a check fails, Changereel does not improvise a narration that sounds plausible. It stops and reports which check failed, on which scene, and what it saw instead. A real log line, unedited:

FAILED at stage Demo: scene[0] screen gate failed: this scene narrates 'driving_depot_to_stop1' but the app is on 'scan', navigation did not reach the screen this scene describes refusing to ship a video it cannot verify

Build versus buy

You could build this. The question is who keeps it alive.

Any strong engineering team can prototype this. The prototype is not the expensive part.

The recording is a fortnight. The refusal is the product.

Driving an emulator and stitching narration onto it demos well within two weeks. Every hard hour after that goes into a system that knows when it is wrong. That work makes the demo look worse before it makes the output trustworthy, which is precisely where internal prototypes stop.

The device layer is where the schedule goes.

From our own logs: a screenshot call that hung for over an hour because nothing bounded it; a snapshot restore that failed while the tooling reported success; an automation handshake that hung for two and a half hours because the screen never went idle behind an animating map; file copies silently truncated by a remount race. None of it is in a tutorial, and none of it is your product.

It is a treadmill, not a project.

The operating system ships, the emulator image moves, the interface toolkit deprecates a call, a model version retires, and your own screens change every sprint. An unmaintained generator does not fade politely. It starts producing confident, wrong videos, which is worse than producing none.

Inside your company, nobody owns this surface.

It spans continuous integration, mobile infrastructure, the teams whose screens it drives, whoever owns product voice, and the people who put it in front of staff. Work that crosses every boundary belongs to none of them, so it becomes a twenty percent project and goes quiet at the next reorganisation.

Improvements arrive by pulling an update.

We run this against real third party apps we did not write, and every failure we find, plus whatever partners choose to tell us, goes back into the system. You get that work by pulling a new version. A team building their own learns from exactly one app, forever.

Measuring yourself honestly is an organisational act.

The number that makes a system like this trustworthy is the rate at which it refuses its own output. Publishing that internally invites the wrong conversation at the wrong moment, so internal tools quietly stop counting. We are paid to keep that number honest and to drive it down.

None of this is beyond your team. It is simply not what you want your best engineers spending the next two quarters on.
